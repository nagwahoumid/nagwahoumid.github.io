About Me

Hi, I'm Nagwa

Software developer and Computer Science graduate from the University of Nottingham (BSc, 2026). I spent 2.5 years as a backend developer at Intelligent Plant in Aberdeen, working in a real Agile team on industrial data software. Open the Experience window for that.

I like building things that are actually useful. Search engines, security tooling, planners that work with your brain instead of against it.

My dissertation, PDF Sherlock, is a fully offline hybrid retrieval system that keeps everything private. It earned a First and it is the kind of work I want to keep doing. Information retrieval and RAG built to respect people's data.

There is one thread running through most of what I do, and that is compression. I love the question of how much you can throw away and still keep the meaning. My dissertation cut vector storage by roughly 75 percent using 8 bit quantisation, and I worked on the same problem with industrial data at Intelligent Plant. Retrieval, compression and quantisation have fascinated me for a long time.

I also go deep at the lowest level with malware analysis, x86 assembly and server security. My habit is to always dig one layer further than the module asks for.

Before Computer Science I did a year of engineering, and that side never left me. I collect electronic components for fun, and I'm planning a masters in electrical engineering next.

When I'm not coding I'm crocheting, hunting for components, or playing chess.

Dissertation ★ First

PDF Sherlock

"An Offline, Privacy Preserving Hybrid Retrieval Architecture for Academic Literature"

BSc dissertation, University of Nottingham, 2026. Graded a First (71).

Most modern retrieval systems push your documents to centralised cloud infrastructure and lean on GPUs. That is a problem for sensitive academic and clinical material. PDF Sherlock runs fully offline on ordinary CPU hardware and keeps every document local.

What it does under the hood

  • Ingestion that understands page layout and reconstructs academic PDFs printed in columns, chunking aligned to text blocks so semantic structure survives parsing
  • Hybrid search, so a sparse BM25 lexical index (Tantivy, memory mapped) fused with dense Sentence-Transformers vectors in a FAISS HNSW graph via Reciprocal Rank Fusion
  • 8-bit Scalar Quantisation cutting dense vector storage by roughly 75 percent, which is the part I enjoyed most, since it is really a question of how much you can compress a vector before the meaning starts to break
  • Evaluated for retrieval effectiveness, statistical significance and computational efficiency on hardware with no GPU

Robotics Lab

Robotics

VoxPoser, evaluated and validated (COMP3088)

For my final year robotics study group I presented VoxPoser, a paper where a language model builds 3D value maps that tell a robot arm where it should move. Rather than only summarising it, I ran my own simulation in CoppeliaSim on my Linux workstation to test whether the architectural claims actually hold up.

My argument was that VoxPoser does not really change control theory, it automates the reference generation layer. The language model decides where to go in task space, and classical controllers still handle joint limits, singularities and stability underneath. I connected the voxel cost maps back to artificial potential fields and pointed out the same weakness, which is local minima where the net force is zero but the goal is never reached.

The lesson that stuck with me came out of running it myself. A plan can be perfectly correct in language and still fail on the robot because of kinematic singularities or controller limits. Semantics and physics are two different problems.

Graded 84.75.
